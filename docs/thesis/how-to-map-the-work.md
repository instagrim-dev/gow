# How to Map the Work

*A reusable recording method. [Why Map the Work?](why-map-the-work.md) lets a
reader experience the reasoning move once; this document turns the move into a
procedure that can be repeated on real problems. It stays at the whiteboard
level — Level 1–2 in the [field guide](when-to-map-the-work.md)'s terms — and
deliberately uses no `newf` machinery.*

The method has four parts, matching the four places the move can silently go
wrong:

1. **Record attempts** so that comparison is possible later.
2. **Distinguish commonality from explanation** so an observed pattern is not
   mistaken for a demonstrated cause.
3. **Design a discriminating probe** so the next attempt tests the explanation
   instead of merely hoping to succeed.
4. **Update the map without overstating the evidence** so the record stays
   honest as it grows.

---

## 1. Record attempts

An attempt is comparable only if it was recorded structurally. A narrative
record ("we tried adding workers, didn't help") cannot be laid side by side
with another narrative; a structural record can.

For each serious attempt, record:

```text
Attempt
  what was varied        the deliberate change, stated as before → after
  what was held fixed    everything inherited from the previous attempt,
                         including things assumed too obvious to matter
  mechanism              why the change was expected to help
  outcome                the measured result, in the same units every time
  verified how           what check established the outcome (test, measurement,
                         inspection, assumption)
```

Two disciplines matter more than the schema:

- **Record "held fixed" honestly.** The payoff of the whole method lives in
  this field. In the worked example, the commit gate's global scope appears in
  every attempt's *held fixed* column — that is the only reason the comparison
  step can find it. The things you don't bother writing down are precisely the
  things every attempt will inherit unexamined.
- **Use the same outcome units across attempts.** "Faster," "better," and
  "still broken" cannot be compared. 40 ticks, 40 ticks, 40 ticks can — and
  the *exact equality* of the three outcomes in the worked example was itself
  the first clue.

Record failures with the same care as successes. A failed attempt whose
structure is recorded narrows the space; a failed attempt remembered as "that
didn't work" narrows nothing.

---

## 2. Distinguish commonality from explanation

With three or more attempts recorded, lay them out as a table: one row per
attempt, one column per structural field, outcome on the right. Then look for:

- **columns that varied** while the outcome stayed fixed — these mechanisms
  are candidates for *doesn't govern the outcome*;
- **columns that never varied** — these are candidates for the unexamined
  restriction;
- **relationships between outcome and structure** — in the worked example,
  ticks = total updates, a functional relationship no single row exposes.

What you have at this point is a **commonality**: a pattern observed in the
attempts on record. It is a hypothesis about the problem, not a fact about it.
Three attempts is a small, biased sample — biased because every attempt was
generated by the same team with the same assumptions, which is exactly why
their shared restriction is informative *and* why their shared pattern might
be coincidence.

The promotion test is: **can the commonality be restated as a claim that
predicts outcomes for configurations not yet tried?**

```text
Commonality      "every attempt kept the global gate; every attempt took 40 ticks"
Explanation      "ticks = the largest number of updates sharing one gate scope"
```

The explanation form earns its status by being quantitative and by making
predictions in *both directions*: where the intervention should help (and by
how much) and where it should not help at all. A restatement that only
predicts "changing the common thing will help" is not yet an explanation — it
is the commonality wearing a lab coat.

---

## 3. Design a discriminating probe

The next attempt should be chosen to **maximize what it teaches**, not to
maximize its chance of success. Those are different objectives, and conflating
them is the default failure mode of "just try again."

A discriminating probe has three properties:

- **It varies the suspected factor and nothing else.** The worked example's
  intervention changes only the gate's scope. Workers, scheduling, update
  count, and stream structure are all held at previously-measured values, so
  any outcome change is attributable.
- **It comes with a numeric prediction made before running.** Not "should be
  faster" but "should be 10 ticks." A prediction stated after the result is
  narration.
- **It is paired with a control that should remove the effect.** The
  single-stream control predicts the intervention's gain vanishes when the
  load cannot divide across scopes. A probe without a control can confirm an
  explanation but cannot discriminate it from its neighbors ("per-stream
  gates are just faster somehow").

And one property of the probe's *evaluation*:

- **Correctness is checked, not assumed.** An intervention that hits the
  predicted number by breaking the invariant the restriction was protecting
  (here: per-stream ordering) is not a success; it is an undetected regression
  with good marketing. Every probe run must re-verify the original
  correctness conditions.

If the probe's outcome matches the prediction in both the intervention and the
control, the explanation survives. If it doesn't, that is not a wasted run —
it is precise information about where your model of the problem is wrong,
which is more than another undirected attempt would have produced.

---

## 4. Update the map without overstating the evidence

After the probe, write down what is now established — at the strength actually
earned and no higher:

```text
established     outcomes measured and correctness-checked, per configuration
explained       the claim, stated with the conditions under which it holds
                AND the conditions under which it was shown not to help
bounded         what was NOT tested (other worker counts? uneven streams?
                different update totals?) — the claim's silent edges
open            the next cheapest probe that would extend or break the claim
```

Three rules keep the record honest:

- **State the claim at the scope it was tested.** "In this model, completion
  time equals the largest per-scope load" — not "gates are the bottleneck in
  concurrent systems." The narrower claim is more useful precisely because
  every part of it was checked.
- **Keep the control in the claim.** The condition under which the
  intervention does nothing is part of the result, not a footnote. It is what
  distinguishes an explanation from an advertisement.
- **Never silently promote.** An explanation that survived one probe is a
  surviving hypothesis, not an established law. It earns further status only
  from further probes it could have failed — ideally probes designed by
  someone trying to break it.

Then the loop closes: the probe itself is a new attempt, recorded with the
same schema as the originals, and the next comparison starts from a richer
table.

```text
record → compare → explain → probe (with control) → update → record …
```

---

## Relationship to the instrumented system

Everything above is executable with a text file and honesty. The reason `newf`
exists is that each step degrades under scale in a specific way: recording
degrades into narrative, comparison degrades into skimming, promotion happens
silently, and controls get skipped when the corpus is large and the operator
is tired. The instrumented system makes each step's artifact durable, typed,
and challengeable — but the discipline it implements is exactly the four-part
method on this page.
